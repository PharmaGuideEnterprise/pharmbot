### Introduction

The following is an overview of drug use during pregnancy. The reader should seek additional or confirmatory information if necessary as it is not a comprehensive review.

### Principles of Prescribing in Pregnancy

Many pregnant patients are exposed to a variety of medications that may exert therapeutic, toxic or teratogenic (i.e., developmentally toxic) effects on the fetus. Since the thalidomide disaster, many physicians and pregnant patients tend to withhold any medication during pregnancy, although the risk of teratogenic effect from most drugs in therapeutic doses is nonexistent. Major congenital defects occur in 1?3% of the general population at birth.[[1]](#ci710n00028) Of the major defects, about 25% are of genetic origin (genetically inherited diseases, new mutations and chromosomal abnormalities) and 65% are of unknown etiology (multifactorial, polygenic, spontaneous errors of development and synergistic interactions of teratogens). Only 2?3% of malformations are thought to be associated with drug treatment. The remaining defects are related to other environmental exposures including infectious agents, toxic chemicals, pregnant patients? disease states, mechanical problems and irradiation.[[2]](#ci710n00006)[[3]](#ci710n00029)

Optimal prescribing in pregnancy is a challenge and should provide maximal safety to the fetus as well as therapeutic benefit to the pregnant patient. To date, very few drugs are proven teratogens in humans. However, drug-induced malformations are important because they are potentially preventable.

Physiologic changes during pregnancy may alter the pharmacokinetics of drugs. Clearance rates of many drugs increase during late pregnancy due to increases in both renal and hepatic elimination (e.g., digoxin, phenytoin). Other drugs (e.g., theophylline) undergo decreased clearance.[[4]](#ParienteGLeibsonTCarlsAEtAl.Pregnan-E4ABA736) Generally, little is known about the relationship between the pregnant patient?s serum drug concentration and risk of teratogenicity. Several drugs known to be teratogenic (e.g., topiramate, valproic acid, lithium) were shown to have a threshold above which they pose a higher teratogenic risk.[[5]](#Koren2019)

The importance of **timing of drug exposure** is better understood. The effect produced by a teratogenic agent depends upon the developmental stage at which the conceptus is exposed. Several important phases in human development are recognized:[[3]](#ci710n00029)

- The **?all-or-none? period**, the time from conception until somite formation, corresponds to the first 17 days after conception. Insults to the embryo in this phase are likely to result in either death, miscarriage or intact survival. At this stage, the embryo is undifferentiated, and repair and recovery are possible through multiplication of the still totipotential cells. Consider that exposure to teratogens during this presomitic stage usually does not cause congenital malformations unless the agent persists in the body beyond this period.[[3]](#ci710n00029)[[6]](#ci710n00081)
- The **embryonic period**, from 18?60 days after conception, is when the basic steps in organogenesis occur. This is the period of maximum sensitivity to teratogenicity since tissues are differentiating rapidly and damage becomes irreparable. Exposure to teratogenic agents during this period has the greatest likelihood of causing a structural anomaly. The pattern of anomalies produced depends on which systems are differentiating at the time of teratogenic exposure.
- The **fetal phase**, from the end of the embryonic period to term, is when growth and functional maturation of formed organs and systems occurs. Teratogen exposure in this period will affect fetal growth (e.g., intrauterine growth restriction) and the size or function of an organ, rather than cause gross structural anomalies. The term *fetal toxicity* is commonly used to describe such an effect.

The potential effect of psychoactive agents (e.g., antidepressants, antiepileptics, alcohol and other drugs of abuse) on the developing CNS has led to the new field of behavioural teratology. Here, the damage to the fetus is not morphological, but rather neurobehavioural, therefore affecting things such as IQ, language development and behaviours.

Many organ systems continue structural and functional maturation long after birth. As a classical example, adenocarcinomas associated with first-trimester exposure to diethylstilbestrol occurred many years later (most commonly in exposed female?s late teens or early 20s).

Typically, teratogens must reach the developing conceptus in sufficient amounts to cause their effects. Large molecules with a molecular weight greater than 1000 (e.g., heparin) do not easily cross the placenta into the embryonic-fetal bloodstream. Other factors influencing the rate and extent of placental transfer of drugs include polarity, lipid solubility and the existence of specific carrier proteins (e.g., P-glycoprotein), which may actively pump drugs from the fetus back to the pregnant patient. However, it is becoming apparent that teratogenicity may be caused by placental damage, even without fetal exposure to the teratogenic agent, as is the case with hypertension.

Since 2015, the FDA has implemented labelling requirements for pregnancy and breastfeeding for all new drug submissions in the United States. The updated format removes the letter-based categorization of risk system (A, B, C, D, X) and provides more complete information pertaining to assessing risk versus benefit of medication use in pregnancy and breastfeeding and in males and females of reproductive potential. Labelling changes for medications submitted on or after June 30, 2001 are being phased in gradually. Medications approved prior to June 30, 2001 are required to remove the letter category but are not subject to the new narrative labelling requirements. Labelling for nonprescription medications is not affected.[[7]](#ci710n00130) Some manufacturers may also decide to follow these FDA labelling requirements in their Canadian product monographs.

### Principles of Teratology Counselling

- Ascertain the clinical facts regarding the nature of the exposure: the length, dosage and timing during pregnancy, as well as other exposures of concern, e.g., alcohol, cigarette smoking, herbal remedies.
- Collect all available current data regarding the agent and the risk of exposure. Examples of resources include:
  - Briggs GG, Freeman RK, Towers CV et al. *Drugs in pregnancy and lactation: a reference guide to fetal and neonatal risk*. 11th ed. Philadelphia (PA): Wolters Kluwer; 2017
  - [Reprotox](http://reprotox.org/)
  - [MotherToBaby](https://mothertobaby.org/)
- Counselling should include background human baseline risk for major malformations, whether the fetus is at increased risk, which anomaly has been associated with the agent in question, a risk assessment, methods of prenatal detection when available, limitations in scientific knowledge and limitations of prenatal diagnostic capabilities.
- Additional considerations include the potential risk of the medical condition for which a drug is prescribed, known interactions between the disease state and the pregnancy, and preventive measures when applicable (e.g., folic acid supplementation in carbamazepine exposure).
- Because more than 50% of pregnancies are unplanned, teratogenic risk assessment should ideally be started prior to pregnancy.
- Undertake counselling in the context of the family's attitudes, beliefs, religion and other cultural components and in a language that they understand. Including clergy, social workers, psychologists or psychiatrists in the process may be helpful to enable the patient and family to fully comprehend the information and ideally make informed decisions.

## Latex

|  |
| --- |
| Antony J. Ham Pong, MBBS, FRCPC |
| Date of Revision: October 19, 2021 |

Allergy to natural rubber latex (NRL), or simply ?latex,? is an important health concern, especially for health-care workers and patients who undergo multiple surgeries. In the 1980s?1990s, a global latex allergy epidemic occurred due to increased exposure to latex, triggered by a >100-fold increase in the use of latex gloves that were widely adopted by the health and food industries as part of universal health precautions to reduce transmission of emerging pathogens such as HIV and hepatitis C. Coupled with enhanced allergenicity of latex gloves due to manufacturing changes implemented to cope with the increased demand, consequent sensitization occurred.[[1]](#KellyKJSussmanG.LatexAllergyWhereAr-7CA0E399) The prevalence of NRL allergy has been estimated to be 9.7% among health-care providers and 7.2% among susceptible patients (e.g., those undergoing surgery or catheterization and those with spina bifida).[[2]](#refitem-162111-583029AD) However, intensive research into understanding the etiology of the latex epidemic has lead to measures that have significantly reduced the incidence of latex allergy in the general population to 1%. These measures have included switching to nonpowdered latex or synthetic gloves as well as avoiding latex exposure in high-risk populations. Reactions to NRL products range from mild to life-threatening or fatal. The severity is dependent on individual susceptibility and the route of NRL-allergen exposure. Mucosal, visceral and parenteral exposures are associated with the greatest risk for severe systemic reactions.[[3]](#ci713n00184) These reactions can be divided into 3 groups:[[4]](#ci713n00185)[[5]](#ci713n00197)

- **Nonallergic irritant contact dermatitis:** a nonimmunologic reaction to chemical additives used during the process of manufacturing rubber from latex. Symptoms are due to mechanical irritation and characterized by redness in lighter skin; dark brown, purple or grey coloration in darker skin; and scaling, cracking, dryness, itching, and vesicle formation on exposed areas.
- **Allergic contact dermatitis:** a delayed type IV allergic reaction to rubber chemicals used in the manufacturing of NRL products. Typical presentation occurs 24?48 hours after contact and is characterized by redness in lighter skin; dark brown, purple or grey coloration in darker skin; and itching and vesicle formation on exposed areas. Repeated contact may cause the skin to become dry, cracked and thickened. Reaction can also occur with synthetic gloves.
- **Allergic immunoglobulin E (IgE)?mediated reaction:** an immediate type I allergic reaction due to residual NRL proteins in NRL products. Urticaria, flushing, edema, rhinitis, conjunctivitis, asthma and anaphylaxis can occur within minutes of exposure.

The **risk of NRL allergy** seems to increase with early and repeated exposure to NRL products. High-risk individuals include patients with spina bifida or other congenital abnormalities who require multiple surgical interventions (especially children). Other groups at risk include health-care workers, rubber industry workers and atopic individuals.[[4]](#ci713n00185)[[6]](#ci713n00187)[[7]](#ci713n00188) The following characteristics may be used to screen for adults and children who may be sensitive to NRL:[[4]](#ci713n00185)

- Contact urticaria, rhinoconjunctivitis, swelling, asthma or anaphylaxis after contact with NRL products, e.g., gloves, condoms, balloons
- History of eczema or atopic diseases
- Allergic reaction during medical, dental or surgical procedures
- Allergies to foods, especially raw tropical fruits, occur in 50% of latex-allergic individuals. This association is known as the latex-fruit syndrome. Cross-reactive plant allergens common to latex and some fruits cause a higher risk of allergies to these foods (e.g., latex allergen Hev b 2 with banana, Hev b 6 with chestnut and avocado, Hev b 5 with kiwi and many other fruits with other latex proteins).[[8]](#ci713n00198) Conversely, a fruit allergy is not an indication to avoid latex in a particular individual, as the risk of a latex allergy in this situation is only 10%[[1]](#KellyKJSussmanG.LatexAllergyWhereAr-7CA0E399)

Patients at high risk of NRL allergy should be referred to a specialist for consultation and confirmatory tests.[[9]](#ci713n00189)

Management and prevention of NRL allergy involves completely avoiding NRL products.[[10]](#ci713n00190)[[11]](#ci713n00191) Precautions must be taken during preparation and administration of parenteral products. Review all materials (e.g., vials, syringes, tip caps, evacuated containers, tubing, minibags, infusion sets, gloves) for NRL content and select NRL-free products when available.

The risk of exposure to trace amounts of NRL from medication vial stoppers is unclear.[[11]](#ci713n00191) NRL vial stoppers are dry NRL products that have less residual NRL protein and have been reported to elicit allergic reactions less frequently than dipped NRL products (medical gloves, condoms, catheters).[[3]](#ci713n00184) However, due to isolated reports of rubber vial stoppers causing allergic reactions,[[12]](#ci713n00192)[[13]](#ci713n00193) many health-care institutions have adopted guidelines to prepare NRL-safe parenteral products.

Two examples are:

- Vial stopper removal: the benefit must be weighed against the potential for dosing errors, dilution problems, contamination and waste.[[6]](#ci713n00187)
- One-stick policy: limit vial access to 1 puncture for any vial stopper. Once the drug is withdrawn, change the needle before administration.[[3]](#ci713n00184)[[14]](#ci713n00196) Some institutions may use a 2-stick policy in specific cases such as oncology medications.[[15]](#BCCancerAgency.BCCAPharmacyDirectiv-58310BAA)

These procedures do not eliminate the possibility that NRL proteins may leach from NRL vial stoppers into the drug solution during storage.[[3]](#ci713n00184) It is best to use latex-free products if possible. Butyl rubber, a synthetic rubber that does not contain NRL, has been used as a substitute.

### How to Find Information about Latex in Product Monographs

Information about latex content in specific products can be located in the following sections of individual *CPS* product monographs:

- Summary Product Information
- Contraindications
- Dosage Forms, Composition and Packaging
- Supplied

Online CPS users can also search for the term latex within a product monograph or across multiple product monographs by entering the product name (or names) in the main Search field, then entering ?latex? in the Find Keyword field.

Do not assume latex is not present if it is not listed in the manufacturer's product monograph. CPhA strongly recommends that you contact the manufacturer directly for confirmatory information (see Manufacturer Directory in the Resources section of CPS online).

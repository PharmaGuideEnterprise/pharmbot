## Nonmedicinal Ingredients

|  |
| --- |
| Antony J. Ham Pong, MBBS, FRCPC |
| Date of Revision: November 4, 2024 |

In addition to active drugs, pharmaceutical products may also contain nonmedicinal ingredients (NMIs) used as diluents, binders, emulsifiers, lubricants, buffers, antioxidants, fillers, preservatives, flavours, colouring agents, sweeteners and for many other functions. Some raw materials used in the manufacturing process are obtained from sources other than the pharmaceutical industry; therefore, the drug manufacturer may be unaware of unlabelled NMIs. For example, agents used to whiten powders may contain traces of sulfites, and dyes used to imprint names on capsules may contain traces of tartrazine or sulfites.

A small percentage of individuals prone to allergic reactions or with particular sensitivities or dietary restrictions may experience adverse effects to some NMIs. Adverse effects may range from mild to life-threatening. Types of adverse effects to NMIs include IgE-mediated (immediate) hypersensitivity reactions, non-IgE-mediated (delayed) hypersensitivity reactions, and interactions with medical conditions or drug therapy. Immediate hypersensitivity reactions may lead to life-threatening anaphylaxis. They typically occur within minutes to a few hours after exposure but may be delayed for as long as 8 hours. Delayed hypersensitivity reactions typically develop 24 hours or more after exposure and are generally not life-threatening. Awareness of the NMI content of pharmaceuticals assists the health-care practitioner in providing advice on product selection for both prescription and nonprescription products in affected patients.

Specific NMIs discussed in this document include carboxymethylcellulose (and its derivative, croscarmellose), corn, ethanol, food dyes, gelatin, gluten, lactose, peanut oil, polyethylene glycol (PEG), soybean oil, soya lecithin and sulfites.

### How to Find Information about Nonmedicinal Ingredients

Finding accurate information about the presence of NMIs in specific pharmaceutical products can be challenging. Although various organizations maintain lists of products containing or free of specific NMIs (see [Suggested Readings](#ci707n00182)) and manufacturers? product monographs can be consulted, CPhA strongly recommends that the manufacturer be contacted directly for each product in question because synonyms or modifications of ingredient names may be used, and NMIs for a specific product may change over time if the manufacturing process changes. See Pharmaceutical Manufacturers/Distributors in the Directories section of *CPS* and [Table 1](#CommonNonmedicinalIngredients-7FC1F5D3).

Within individual *CPS* product monographs, information about nonmedicinal ingredients in specific products may be located in any of the following sections: Summary Product Information, Contraindications, Dosage Forms, Composition and Packaging, Supplied. Online users can also search *CPS* for a specific NMI within a product monograph or across multiple product monographs by entering the product name (or names) in the main search field, then, under *Results*, entering the NMI (e.g., ?lactose?) in the *Filter by Keyword* field. Note that product lists described as ?clinically relevant nonmedicinal ingredients? do not necessarily include all NMIs.

### Carboxymethylcellulose and Croscarmellose

Carboxymethylcellulose (CMC or cellulose gum) is widely used as a binder, suspending agent or thickening agent, or to stabilize emulsions in tablets, foods and cosmetics; dental products such as toothpastes; barium enemas; and injectable preparations. Croscarmellose (carmellose) is a derivative of CMC that is used as a binding agent, suspending agent, disintegrant (to improve drug dissolution and disintegration) and as an active bulk laxative. Both CMC and croscarmellose have been implicated in rare but serious IgE-mediated (immediate) allergic reactions to pharmaceutical products. Anaphylaxis due to CMC has been reported during barium enema[[1]](#DumondPFranckPMorissetMEtAl.Pre-let-80384AF2) and with injectable corticosteroids containing CMC, including triamcinolone.[[2]](#BigliardiPLIzakovicJWeberJMEtAl.Ana-803801B7) Croscarmellose has been implicated in anaphylaxis to oral medications.[[3]](#MumoliNCeiMLuschiREtAl.AllergicReac-80387C83) It is possible, though unproven, that these cellulose derivatives and others such as methyl cellulose and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (hypromellose) are cross-reactive; anaphylaxis to hypromellose in ophthalmic products has been reported.[[4]](#MunkSJHeegaardS.MosbechHEtAl.TwoEpi-8038B658)

### Corn

Corn (maize) products may be found as fillers in pharmaceutical products and cosmetics, mainly as corn starch or as sweeteners such as corn syrup and maltodextrin. Corn allergy is rare in North America, but more prevalent in areas and cultures where it is ingested more commonly e.g., southern Europe and Mexico, and by African-American and Hispanic populations.[[5]](#maizeAllergy)[[6]](#g202MaizeCorn) There is currently no requirement for corn to be listed in any country?s labelling regulations; therefore, corn starch may be labelled as food starch. There are few studies of corn allergy and some isolated case reports. Allergenic corn proteins are found mainly in corn kernels and corn flour and much less in corn starch. Highly processed corn by-products such as refined corn oil, dextrose, fructose, sorbitol, glucose, mannitol, ethanol and alcohol have not been reported to cause allergies. Corn allergens may cause contact dermatitis or typical food allergic reactions such as hives, asthma, rhinitis or anaphylaxis with ingestion.[[6]](#g202MaizeCorn)[[7]](#maizeDoubleBlind) Gastrointestinal symptoms such as bloating, cramps and diarrhea may occur from corn syrup due to fructose malabsorption, but the amount of fructose in corn starch and corn-based pharmaceutical sweeteners is too low to provoke these symptoms. Inhalation of corn flour may lead to occupational allergy and asthma and development of allergy to ingested corn.[[6]](#g202MaizeCorn) Food-dependent, exercise-induced anaphylactic reactions to corn have been reported, i.e., allergic reactions only when corn ingestion is combined with exercise.[[8]](#cornAnaphylaxis) Corn allergy has been reported to develop after repetitive use of a soap containing corn, leading to allergic reactions on eating corn.[[9]](#cornSoap) There are isolated case reports of anaphylaxis from contact with cornstarch powder in surgical gloves, as well as contact dermatitis due to corn starch.[[10]](#cornstarchGlove) One patient with corn allergy had challenge-positive skin reactions to an antihistamine containing corn starch.[[11]](#drugOrFoodProb) Allergic reactions to corn appear to be most frequently reported with ingestion of relatively large amounts as a food product, although as low as 100 mg have caused allergic reactions.[[7]](#maizeDoubleBlind) However, reactions to corn in pharmaceutical products appear to be extremely rare.

### Ethanol

Ethanol (also called absolute alcohol, anhydrous alcohol, dehydrated alcohol, ethyl alcohol, ethyl hydrate, ethyl hydroxide) is often used as a preservative and solvent in liquid pharmaceutical preparations. It may also be included in some products for its sedative effects. According to some sources, the concentration of ethanol in prescription or nonprescription products can be up to 40% or more, resulting in significant alcohol intake over the course of a day, depending on the volume of medication taken.[[12]](#ParkerWA.Alcohol-containingPharmace-7FC756C5)

Awareness of the ethanol content of medications is important since avoidance of ethanol, or use of low-ethanol-content products, is important in:

- Patients with a medical condition that requires alcohol avoidance
- Patients taking a medication that may interact with alcohol (consult a reliable drug interaction reference)
- Pregnant patients (there is no known safe amount of alcohol use during pregnancy)
- Children (they are not able to metabolize ethanol as efficiently as adults)[[13]](#ZuccottiGVFabianoV.SafetyIssuesWith-7FC3AEB7)
- Patients who wish to avoid alcohol for religious or personal reasons

### Food Dyes

Natural and synthetic food dyes are widely used in the pharmaceutical and food industries to colour products for attractiveness or identification purposes. Adverse reactions to food dyes are rare, estimated to occur in 0.01?0.23% of the population.[[14]](#FeketeaGTsabouriS.CommonFoodColoran-95665834) Natural food dyes (e.g., annatto, Carmine Red) are more commonly used in foods rather than pharmaceuticals, and can lead to IgE-mediated (immediate) reactions, including anaphylaxis, due to their protein content.

Commonly used synthetic dyes include tartrazine (FD&C Yellow No. 5, Sunset Yellow), Allura Red (FD&C Red No. 40) and erythrosine (FD&C Red No. 3). The mechanism behind reactions to synthetic food dyes is unclear, but anaphylactic reactions are rare. Tartrazine sensitivity is no longer felt to occur at a higher frequency in ASA- and NSAID-sensitive individuals.[[15]](#ArdernKDRamFS.TartrazineExclusionFo-956282E4) The most commonly reported reactions to synthetic food dyes include worsening of atopic eczema, urticaria and angioedema, and asthma. Tartrazine and, to a lesser extent, other synthetic dyes, have been implicated in behavioural changes in children. A small subset of children may have short-term behavioural changes linked to consumption of food dyes but this remains a controversial topic. Adverse reactions attributed to food dyes in medications, especially urticaria, may be coincidental, due to reactions to the medication itself (e.g., antibiotics, NSAIDs) or the underlying illness (e.g., viral infections in children).[[14]](#FeketeaGTsabouriS.CommonFoodColoran-95665834)

### Gelatin

Gelatin is a protein derived from hydrolysis of collagen from beef or porcine hide or bones. It is used to make capsules and gelcaps, as well as in tablets, suspensions, suppositories and laxative jellies as a gelling agent. It may be found as a component in some vaccines as a stabilizing agent. Gelatin allergy is a rare, IgE-mediated (immediate) reaction that may be a cause of unexplained anaphylaxis with ingestion or injection of gelatin-containing medications or vaccines. People who have allergic reactions to gelatin in medications may also react to gelatin-containing foods such as jellies, gummy candies, marshmallows, deli meats and frozen desserts.[[16]](#LandMHPiehlMDBurksAW.NearFatalAnaph-803C3CF1)[[17]](#FranceschiniFBottauPCaimmiSEtAl.Vac-803C7F43) However, many individuals who experience allergic reactions to gelatin-containing vaccines can ingest small amounts of gelatin orally in food or medications without reactions. Those with a history of allergic reactions to gelatin-containing foods must avoid gelatin-containing products. Some patients may wish to avoid gelatin ingestion for religious or personal reasons (e.g., vegan, vegetarian).

### Gluten

Gluten is a mixture of 2 proteins (gliadin and glutenin) found in wheat, barley, rye and triticale. Gluten is not present in rice, corn or oats; however, oats are generally avoided unless labelled as ?pure and uncontaminated oats? because of gluten cross-contamination during processing. Gluten is not generally intentionally added to pharmaceuticals but could be present in some starches used as fillers or in thickening agents derived from starches.

Celiac disease is a non-IgE-mediated (delayed) hypersensitivity reaction to the gliadin fraction of ingested gluten, resulting in immunological inflammatory damage to the lining of the small intestine in genetically predisposed individuals. The inflammation may lead to malabsorption of nutrients, fluids and electrolytes by reducing the amount of surface area available for absorption. Depending on the extent and severity of the mucosal inflammation, patients may be asymptomatic or exhibit a wide range of GI symptoms (e.g., diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal pain, discomfort) and non-GI effects (e.g., iron-deficiency anemia, osteoporosis, dermatitis herpetiformis). The treatment for celiac disease is a strict lifetime avoidance of gluten ingestion. Since even a small amount of gluten can be problematic for the individual with celiac disease, it is recommend that patients avoid gluten-containing medications administered not only orally but also rectally, as the rectal mucosa has been shown to be sensitive to topical exposure to gluten.[[18]](#EnsariAMarshMNMorganSEtAl.Diagnosin-54ED1323) Persistent nonadherence to a gluten-free diet may also increase the risk of certain cancers.[[19]](#ci707n00198)

### Lactose

Lactose is a large sugar molecule found in milk and dairy products. It is used as a diluent, bulking agent and filler for solid dosage forms such as tablets and capsules. Lactose is broken down by the intestinal enzyme lactase to glucose and galactose, which are easily absorbed in the GI tract. Lactase deficiency leads to undigested lactose in the gut, which is then broken down by bacteria instead, causing symptoms including abdominal cramps, diarrhea, distension and flatulence. Although the amount of lactose in 1 tablet or capsule is small compared to the amount found in dairy products, symptoms can occur in lactose-intolerant patients who take multiple lactose-containing medications.

Primary lactose intolerance is a common, inherited deficiency of lactase that develops as someone?s diet becomes less reliant on milk and dairy (usually in childhood, though symptoms may not occur until adulthood). Secondary lactose intolerance is a temporary or permanent condition that may occur in individuals who have had an intestinal infection or gastric surgery, have an inflammatory bowel condition, or following the use of certain medications (e.g., antibiotics, cancer chemotherapy, anti-inflammatory agents). Exogenous ingestion of the lactase enzyme can increase lactose tolerance in affected individuals. Lactase is available as drops, tablets or caplets. For more information, consult the *Compendium of Products for Minor Ailments: Nutrition Products, Lactose Digestants*.

Lactose is also contraindicated in individuals with the fructose-galactose malabsorption syndrome called galactosemia. Another potential concern is the possible contamination of pharmaceutical-grade lactose (and lactose-derived lactulose) with trace amounts of milk proteins. Although it?s generally not considered sufficient to cause a reaction in persons with a cow?s milk allergy, there have been anecdotal reports.[[20]](#GovernmentOfxa0Canada.HealthProduct-0B55EA31) Similarly, milk-allergic asthmatic individuals may be at risk of allergic reactions when inhaling lactose in dry powder inhalers (DPIs) as demonstrated by 1 well-documented case report.[[21]](#ci707n00199) Some DPIs may contain lactose; consult individual product monographs. Pressurized metered-dose inhalers do not contain lactose.

### Peanut Oil

Peanut oil (also known as arachis oil) is derived directly from the kernels of the peanut plant, a member of the legume family. Peanut oil is used as a solubilizing agent in liquid pharmaceutical products including injections, oral solutions or capsules, and topical products. There are many allergens in peanuts that provoke IgE-mediated (immediate) hypersensitivity reactions. In highly sensitized individuals, trace quantities of these allergens can induce an anaphylactic reaction. The peanut oil used in pharmaceutical products is generally highly refined and most of the peanut protein is removed during manufacturing; however, avoidance of heat-refined arachis oil/peanut oil by individuals with a peanut allergy remains controversial since there is conflicting evidence on whether residual proteins in these oils can provoke allergic reactions. Heat-refined, protein-free arachis oil/peanut oil should not cause allergic reactions in individuals with a peanut allergy[[22]](#HealthCanadax2019sPositionOnHighlyR-89FF6347) but those individuals using drug products containing peanut oils should verify with the manufacturer that the peanut oil is protein-free or contains too small an amount of protein to pose a health risk. Management of anaphylactic peanut allergies involves completely avoiding medications that contain peanut products or incompletely refined peanut/arachis oils.

### Polyethylene Glycol (PEG)

Polyethylene glycol (PEG or macrogol) is a petroleum-based synthetic product used widely in pharmaceutical manufacturing as a dispersing agent, surfactant, solvent, plasticizer, lubricant in oral and liquid products, and as a base for suppositories and topical products including cosmetics. It is also used directly as a laxative, including as part of bowel preparation protocols before procedures such as colonoscopy. There have been reports of IgE-mediated (immediate) hypersensitivity reactions to PEG exposure via injectable corticosteroids, oral analgesics and colonic cleansing agents.[[23]](#WylonKDolleSWormM.PolyethyleneGlyco-824BBF0D)[[24]](#LeeSHChaJMLeeJIEtAl.AnaphylacticSho-824BEEB3)[[27]](#StoneCAJrLiuYRellingMVEtAl.Immediat-824C2224)[[28]](#BrockowKGarveyLHAbererWEtAl.SkinTes-824C83D5) Anaphylactic reactions to mRNA COVID-19 vaccines have been postulated to be related to their PEG content, but this has not been confirmed.[[29]](#CanadianSocietyOfAllergyAndClinical-7F3D295E) There have been a few isolated reports of allergic reactions to these vaccines in PEG-allergic individuals; however, PEG-allergic patients have generally tolerated PEG-containing COVID-19 vaccines.[[25]](#covidPEG)[[26]](#pegRisk) Many patients have reported local reactions to PEG in topical products before the onset of systemic symptoms from ingested products. There may be some cross-reactivity between PEG and polysorbate 80, a surfactant and emulsifier used in eye drops and cosmetics and for stabilizing parenteral drugs (including some vaccines).[[27]](#StoneCAJrLiuYRellingMVEtAl.Immediat-824C2224) A history of repeated sensitivity reactions to a variety of seemingly unrelated household and medical products should heighten awareness of possible PEG allergy.

### Soybean Oil and Soya Lecithin

Soybean oil is a vegetable oil extracted from the seeds of the soybean (also known as soy or soya). Soya lecithin is a by-product of soybean oil processing. Soybean oil is used in lipid emulsions for parenteral nutrition and as a carrier for active ingredients in pharmaceutical products. Soya lecithin is used as a dispersing agent, emulsifier, carrier and wetting agent in pharmaceutical manufacturing. Small amounts of soya protein may be found in medications that contain soybean oil or soya lecithin.

Soybeans, like peanuts, belong to the legume family of plants. Individuals with a peanut allergy may develop an IgE-mediated (immediate) soya allergy in 5?15% of cases. However, peanut allergy is not a contraindication to use of soya products unless there is a coexisting soya allergy. Medications that contain unrefined soybean oil or soya lecithin should be avoided in individuals with a severe soya allergy unless otherwise advised by their doctor. Refined soybean oil contains very little residual protein and is not considered a risk for individuals with a soya allergy.[[22]](#HealthCanadax2019sPositionOnHighlyR-89FF6347)

### Sulfites

Sulfites (sulphites) are compounds that contain the sulfite ion and may occur naturally in foods and humans. The term sulfites includes sodium or potassium bisulfite, sodium or potassium metabisulfite, sodium sulfite, and sulfur dioxide. Sulfiting (sulphiting) agents are used as antioxidants in the preservation of foods and drugs.

The mechanism of sulfite-induced bronchospasm and anaphylaxis is unknown in most cases; however, IgE-mediated (immediate) and non-IgE-mediated (delayed) hypersensitivity reactions to sulfites have been reported. Urticaria, nausea, diarrhea, wheezing and dyspnea are reported most frequently after ingestion of foods treated with sulfites, but they also occur after exposure to drug products containing sulfites. The concentration of sulfites in pharmaceuticals is usually low but adverse reactions to sulfites are not always dose-related. Adverse reactions to sulfites, particularly in individuals with asthma, may be life-threatening. In those individuals, sensitivity is more common to inhaled rather than ingested sulfites. Many patients with a ?food allergy? or ?drug allergy? may actually have sulfite sensitivity and be reacting to sulfites contained in the products.
